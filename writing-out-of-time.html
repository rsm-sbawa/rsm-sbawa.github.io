Judge a model on next month's data, not last month's

The fraud model with the best test score wasn't the one I chose. Here's why the out-of-time check changed my mind.

In my fraud analytics course I built a model to flag fraudulent card transactions in a dataset of 98,161 transactions. The goal was practical: if a review team can only check the riskiest 3% of transactions, how much of the fraud does the model put in front of them?

The usual way to check a model is to hide a random slice of the data, train on the rest, and score the hidden slice. That's the test set. The problem is that a random slice comes from the same months the model learned from. Fraud doesn't stand still, and next month's fraud won't look exactly like last month's.

Three scores, not one

So I scored every model three ways: on the data it trained on, on a random test slice, and on a later time period it never saw at all, called out-of-time. Here are the best versions of each model family, measured as the share of fraud caught in the riskiest 3%:

ModelTrainTestOut-of-time
Logistic regression65.5%67.7%49.6%
Decision tree69.3%69.0%54.2%
Random forest79.1%73.4%57.8%
LightGBM78.7%72.0%53.5%
XGBoost78.1%73.8%54.4%

On the test slice, XGBoost looked best at 73.8%. On later data it dropped to 54.4%. Random forest scored slightly lower on the test slice, at 73.4%, but held up best on later data at 57.8%. That's the model I chose, because the later period is the one that looks like real life.

Watching a model memorize

The clearest lesson came from a single decision tree. As I let it grow deeper, its training score climbed toward 100%. Its test score peaked at 69.1% at depth 7 and then slid to 63.3% by depth 30. The tree wasn't learning fraud anymore; it was memorizing the training rows. Out-of-time results fell even further.

What I took from it

  • A random test split can flatter a model when the world changes over time. Hold out the most recent period too.
  • Pick the simplest model that is close to the best and has a small gap between test and later data.
  • Report the out-of-time number. It's lower, but it's the honest one, and it's what the business will actually see.

With random forest, reviewing the riskiest 5% of transactions caught 66.5% of the fraud in the later period, about $165,000 of it. I used the same habit on a second project, application fraud, where LightGBM caught 58.9% of fraud in the riskiest 3% of 1 million applications.
