Why stockouts make your forecast lie

A store that keeps running out of its best sellers will keep buying too little of them. Here's the loop, and how we broke it for a real client.

When a product is out of stock, the sales system records zero sales for that month. That zero looks like data, but it isn't demand. Nobody bought the kit because nobody could. Feed that history into a forecast and the model learns that the product sells less than it really does.

That's exactly what we found in our MSBA capstone for Crafter.com, an online craft-kit company. Some of their top sellers sat out of stock for up to 24 of the 26 months we looked at. Every forecast built on recorded sales told them to order less, so the next stockout came sooner. Revenue kept sliding, and the data seemed to agree that demand was falling.

Step 1: find the zeros that aren't real

We went through 564,780 order line items and flagged months where a product recorded zero sales between months where it was clearly selling. Those interior zeros are the signature of a stockout, not a lack of interest. We treated them as censored observations, meaning the true demand is unknown rather than zero.

Step 2: rebuild what demand would have been

For each of those months we estimated demand from the product's own history and its seasonal pattern. We reconstructed 360 stockout months this way, and only filled in months with a real stockout signal; nothing else was changed. Across the best-selling A-class products, about 6% of demand had been hidden by those zeros.

Six percent sounds small. It isn't, when it sits on exactly the products that bring in most of the revenue. In Crafter's case, 33 of 176 products drive about 80% of revenue, and 62 drive about 95%.

Step 3: turn the forecast into a decision

A forecast on its own doesn't tell anyone what to buy. So we compared Ridge, SARIMA, XGBoost and Random Forest on months the models hadn't seen. Random Forest won, with an out-of-sample R² of 0.45 and about 12% error. We used it to rank every product by expected revenue per dollar spent on inventory. Under a $15,000 budget, the buy list funds 49 orders for about $14,800 and is expected to bring in about $56,300, a 3.8x return.

An R² of 0.45 is not impressive on its own, and we said so. But the useful question was never why R² isn't higher. It was where Crafter should spend its next purchasing dollar, and the buy list answers that every month.

What I'd tell any team that plans from sales history

  • If a product was unavailable, its recorded sales are a floor, not a forecast.
  • Look for interior zeros before you train anything. They're easy to find and expensive to ignore.
  • Track on-hand stock every week. Crafter only had it for 13% of products, which is the single biggest thing holding their accuracy back.
  • Judge the forecast by the decision it improves, not only by its error score.
